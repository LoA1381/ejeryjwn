Through assimilation, the federal government wanted to remove Native culture and make Native people a part of the United States. In class, this idea was explained with the phrase "Kill the Indian, save the man." It meant removing everything tied to Native culture and leaving only a "shell" of the person. One way to do this was boarding schools. Once federal money was set aside for educating Native people, many schools for poor and marginalized people became schools only for Native students.
Law was another way. The Courts of Indian Offenses, formally sanctioned in 1883, used law as a step toward civilizing Native people. Preferred judges read and wrote English and wore "citizens' dress." Polygamists could not serve. Most offenses these courts punished were cultural, like dances, plural marriage and the practices of medicine men. Medicine people were targeted the most because they were knowledge keepers who held the community's stories and ceremonies and could bring people together. Personally, I felt that going after them first shows the government was trying to cut off the culture at its roots.

The plenary power doctrine let Congress control Native lives without tribal consent or a clear constitutional basis. It came out of U.S. v. Kagama in 1886, a murder case under the Major Crimes Act of 1885. Kagama's lawyers argued that the act was beyond the power of Congress since it had nothing to do with commerce, almost the only area where the Constitution mentions tribes.
The Supreme Court agreed that the act was not about commerce but upheld it anyway. It called the tribes "the wards of the nation" and said that "from their very weakness and helplessness... there arises the duty of protection, and with it the power." To me, this turned a duty to protect into a reason to control. The Court also claimed this plenary, or absolute, power belonged to the federal government "because it never has existed anywhere else." The professor compared this to writing "because I said so" in a paper. I felt the same.
The government already had the goal of "Kill the Indian, save the man" but had not known how to reach it. After Kagama, Congress could do almost anything with the tribes, from funding boarding schools to dividing up land without consent.

The Dawes Act of 1887, or General Allotment Act, was at the center of the assimilation agenda. Senator Henry Dawes wanted to make the tribes a constituent part of the United States. He believed what stood in the way was Native people's "unhealthy" relationship with the land, so the law was designed to break that relationship.
The act let the President divide reservation land among individual Native people without tribal consent. Each head of family got 160 acres, held in trust for 25 years as "protection." The land left over was treated as "excess" and sold to non-Native people. In class this was compared to a checkerboard of Native and non-Native land. Dawes said he wanted to help, but selling off so much land makes that hard to believe.
The Curtis Act of 1898 went further by dissolving the governments of the Five Civilized Tribes and basing membership on descent from the final rolls. I find it ironic that many tribes still use those rolls today. The Burke Act of 1906, passed under pressure from timber companies, let the Secretary of the Interior issue patents before the 25 years were up. These decisions were often based on race. Once the land could be sold and taxed, many Native people lost it.
