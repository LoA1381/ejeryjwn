Through the assimilation policy of the 1880s to the 1920s, the federal government wanted to get rid of Native culture and make Native people a part of the United States. In class, the phrase "Kill the Indian, save the man" was introduced as a way of showing how people thought during this time. It means removing as much as possible of anything connected to Native culture and leaving only the person who remains as a "shell." To do this, the government tried to put the reservations and the people living on them under its own control.
One of the tools for this was boarding schools. In the colonial period, during the 1600s and 1700s, there were schools where missionaries taught Christianity to Native people. In the 1800s, schools were also built for marginalized people such as poor white people and former slaves. However, once the federal government started setting aside money for educating Native people, many of those schools turned into boarding schools only for Native students so they could get that funding.
Another tool was the law. When reservations were first set up, non-Native people served as judges in the courts there, but tribal people did not respect them and the courts did not work well. So Indian agents started trying out Native people as judges. In 1883 the Courts of Indian Offenses received formal sanction and got federal funding. Three Native people served as judges, based on the idea of using law as a step toward civilization. Under the rules for appointment, preference was given to those who could read and write English, wore "citizens' dress" and engaged in "civilized pursuits." Polygamists were not allowed to be appointed. This shows that the government was trying to change even their language, clothing, work and family life. However, to get people to accept the courts, the government needed to choose leaders who were respected in the community, so it ended up breaking its own rules. There was even a case where a man with seven wives became a judge.
The acts that were punished included dances, plural marriage, the practices of medicine men, immorality and intoxication. Most of these were cultural practices that would not normally be thought of as crimes. Medicine people were targeted the most. According to the professor, the idea that medicine people use witchcraft is just a stereotype. In reality they were knowledge keepers who passed down stories, traditions, ceremonies and ways of healing sickness. They also had the power to bring people together. Because of this, they could be put in jail for about 30 days if they were caught. Personally, I felt that going after the people who held the community's knowledge first clearly shows that the goal of this policy went beyond changing their way of life to cutting off the culture itself.
Also, the issue of land was tied up with assimilation. While the government said it was "helping" Native people, the land left over after allotment was sold to non-Native people as "surplus land."

The congressional plenary power doctrine made it possible for Congress to step into the lives of Native people without the consent of the tribes and without any clear basis in the Constitution. The foundation for this was U.S. v. Kagama in 1886.
After the Crow Dog case, Congress passed the Major Crimes Act in 1885 to bring crime under its own control. The Kagama case involved two Native people from Northern California who were indicted under this law for committing murder. The issue was whether the Major Crimes Act was outside the power of Congress. The Constitution mentions tribes almost only in the Indian Commerce Clause, so Kagama's lawyers argued that the Major Crimes Act had nothing to do with commerce and that the power of Congress was not in the Constitution.
The Supreme Court agreed that the Major Crimes Act was not about commerce. Even so, it decided that the law was within the power of Congress. The basis for this was the guardianship relationship that first appeared in Cherokee Nation v. Georgia. The ruling called the tribes "the wards of the nation" and "communities dependent on the United States." It said that "from their very weakness and helplessness... there arises the duty of protection, and with it the power." What the professor emphasized was this "power" part. Because there is a responsibility to protect, there is also the power to protect. This meant that Congress could change its policies whenever it wanted, such as by passing laws to send children to boarding schools or by setting aside money for them.
This power is called the plenary power doctrine. "Plenary" means absolute. This case was the first time the doctrine appeared in Indian law. The ruling also says the power belongs to the federal government because "it never has existed anywhere else," because "it has never been denied" and because "it alone can enforce its laws on all the tribes." The professor said this was like writing "because I said so" in a paper, which was the same impression I had. This kind of reasoning, which puts its basis outside the Constitution, is called extra-constitutional authority. Personally, I felt that the logic of "there is a duty, so there is power" replaces the rights of the people who are supposed to be protected with the control of the people doing the protecting. The ruling also calls the tribes "these remnants of a race once powerful, now weak and diminished in numbers." Around this time, court cases began to describe tribes as a race. This case was the first example of that.
After this ruling, Congress was able to do almost anything it wanted with the tribes. The government had the goal of "Kill the Indian, save the man," but it did not know how to actually make that happen. However, once plenary power was recognized, it could try out all kinds of policies on the reservations. The Major Crimes Act, the Dawes Act and this ruling can be read together as one set. Funding for boarding schools and land allotment carried out without any provision for tribal consent were only possible because of this power.

The Dawes Act of 1887, also called the General Allotment Act, was a law at the center of the assimilation policy. The goal of Henry Dawes, the senator from Massachusetts who sponsored the bill, was to make the tribes a constituent part of the United States. Dawes was someone who truly wanted to help Native people. He believed that what stood in the way was Native people's "unhealthy" relationship with the land. The idea was that if that relationship could be broken, the path to civilization would open up. In other words, the purpose of this law was to break the relationship between Native people and the land.
The Dawes Act gave the President the power to allot reservation land to individual Native people. There was no provision for the consent of the tribes or of individuals. Male heads of household received 160 acres, though on smaller reservations it could be 80 or 40 acres. People sent by the President surveyed the reservations and decided on the allotments. The land that was left over was then sold to non-Native people as "surplus land." In class this was compared to a checkerboard, which made it easy to picture how Native land and non-Native land ended up scattered and mixed together inside the reservations. The reservation boundaries still exist today, but because there is non-Native land inside them, problems have come up for tribes over things like regulating the land. The allotted land was held in trust for 25 years as "protection." During that time Native people did not hold the deed and had to prove they could use the land as farmland. In the end, many people apparently never got to own it. Dawes said he was helping Native people, but when you think about how much surplus land was sold, that claim is questionable.
Allotment also reached into the political structure of the tribes. The tribes that had been removed to Oklahoma had been promised during removal that they would never have to give up any more land, so the government had a hard time getting them to agree to allotment. The Final Rolls of Citizens and Freedmen of the Five Civilized Tribes were also meant to convince them to agree to an allotment plan, but it was difficult to get individuals to participate. So the Curtis Act of 1898 created a new roll that would supersede all others, included a provision that dissolved tribal governments and made membership depend on proving descent from a person listed on the final rolls. The idea of blood quantum also came out of this allotment process. Many tribes still use these rolls today to decide who their members are. Personally, I found it very ironic that documents made under an assimilation policy meant to get rid of Indians are still used by the tribes themselves today.
The Burke Act of 1906 also pushed the loss of land forward. Some allotments had valuable resources like timber on them, but because of the 25-year trust period, no one could get to them. So timber companies and others pressured Congress, which led to this law. The Burke Act let the Secretary of the Interior issue a patent before the 25 years were up, which made the land subject to sale, encumbrance and taxation. The standards the Secretary used to review patents were racial. "Mixed-blood" people were seen as more responsible because they had white blood. Apparently they even went as far as checking people's hair to decide. People who received patents sold their land to companies, gave it away for very little because they were desperate or simply lost it. As the professor said, the Burke Act and the Dawes Act worked together as a set.
